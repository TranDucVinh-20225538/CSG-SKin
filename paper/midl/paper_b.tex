\begin{abstract}
Domain-adversarial training (DANN) is often used to remove site and device shortcuts in
medical imaging models, and deployed models are expected to have an out-of-distribution
(OOD) monitor that flags unfamiliar inputs. We study how DANN affects such monitors in skin
lesion classification, using a lesion-disjoint ISIC~2019 split, smartphone images as the
second domain and a dual-encoder model with two backbone families (EfficientNet-B3 and DINOv2-S/14). With gradient reversal, Mahalanobis OOD detection drops from AUROC $0.825$ to
$0.381$ ($95\%$ CI $[0.358, 0.404]$) on held-out smartphone images, which then look more
in-distribution than the model's own test images. The same happens on three clinical datasets that were not used in
training (Fitzpatrick17k $0.405$, MED-NODE $0.288$, SD-198 $0.376$), while in-distribution balanced
accuracy changes little (paired change $-0.009$, $95\%$ CI $[-0.026, +0.006]$) and in-distribution
ECE stays at $0.20$--$0.21$. Our
interventions point to label shift between the two domains as the main driver in this setting. When we change only the class
mix that the adversary sees, AUROC on the external datasets changes by $0.24$--$0.37$, and the
association remains after adjusting for measured domain leakage. Multi-kernel MMD alignment at similar
leakage also inverts detection on the smartphone images, while CORAL does not. DANN moves unfamiliar images toward the
majority class. Under label shift, a standard domain probe then shows chance-level leakage,
although the domain can still be read within each class. The clinical cost is concrete: in the same models, the share of melanomas from unseen sources
that the classifier calls benign nevus with confidence above $0.9$ increases from $13\%$ to
$51$--$81\%$, and the monitor accepts almost all of these images with or without adaptation.
Correcting for the class priors during training recovers detection only in part when the
priors are estimated without labels; in a post hoc analysis, priors from $50$ labelled target images bring detection
to the level obtained with the true priors, at a cost in in-distribution accuracy. A single-encoder DANN did not reach domain invariance, so these results do not show that every DANN inverts detection. We recommend checking OOD detection again after domain adaptation and
measuring domain leakage within class.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Skin lesion classifiers often learn acquisition artefacts such as ink markings, rulers,
dermatoscope type and contributing site, because these correlate with diagnosis in public
datasets~\cite{winkler2019,bissoto2020,daneshjou2022}. A common remedy is to make the
representation invariant to the acquisition domain, usually with domain-adversarial
training and a gradient reversal layer~\cite{ganin2016,bousmalis2016}. Deployed models are
also expected to have an out-of-distribution (OOD) monitor, which flags inputs that differ
from the training data so that a clinician can review them. We study how invariance
training affects this monitor.

We find that it can invert the monitor. When the adversarial weight increases, OOD detection does
not just get worse but falls below chance, so held-out images from the aligned domain and images from external sources
get scores that look more in-distribution than the model's own test images. Standard
evaluation does not show this (Section~\ref{sec:lesionsplit}), and the domain-leakage probe, which the method is
supposed to reduce, goes down. In the same models, melanomas from external sources are
predicted as benign nevus with confidence above $0.9$ four to six times more often than
without adaptation; the monitor accepts almost all of these images with or without
adaptation, so the increased risk of a missed malignant case comes from the classifier, not
from the monitor.

In our experiments, label shift between the aligned domains is the main driver. ISIC~2019 is $50.8\%$ nevus while the smartphone images used as the
second domain are $12.3\%$ nevus. When two domains have different class priors, matching
their marginal feature distributions forces the model to misplace the classes of one
domain~\cite{zhao2019invariant,tachet2020domain}. We test what this does to OOD monitoring by
changing only the class mix that the adversary sees, and we look at where the OOD images
end up in feature space.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Under a lesion-disjoint split, gradient reversal inverts Mahalanobis OOD detection on
held-out smartphone images and on three clinical datasets that were not used in training.
This holds for two training
recipes, both within $0.08$ balanced accuracy of a standard ERM baseline on the same split, and for a dual-encoder model with a DINOv2-S/14 backbone. All models showing this are dual-encoder models; a single-encoder DANN did not reach invariance and was not tested.
\item When the class mix seen by the adversary is set to that of the target domain, the
inversion on external domains disappears (AUROC difference $0.24$--$0.37$). In a regression
that adjusts for measured domain leakage, the label-shift effect remains, so it is not only a
matter of deeper invariance.
\item DANN moves images that differ from the source along the domain direction toward the
majority class. Under label shift, an unweighted domain probe gives chance-level leakage
even though a within-class probe can still read the domain, so we recommend measuring
leakage within class.
\item Importance weighting with label-free prior estimates recovers most of the lost AUROC at
$\lam=0.5$ without loss of in-distribution accuracy, less at $\lam=2$, and not on MED-NODE. In a post hoc analysis, priors from $50$ labelled target images give point estimates close to
those with the true priors, but in-distribution accuracy drops.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Shortcuts and invariance in dermatology.}
Ink markings shift melanoma predictions~\cite{winkler2019}, classifiers keep competitive
ISIC accuracy with the lesion masked out~\cite{bissoto2020}, and performance degrades on
darker skin~\cite{daneshjou2022}. Domain-adversarial training~\cite{ganin2016} and domain
separation networks~\cite{bousmalis2016} are the usual remedies; our dual-encoder model is
a domain separation network and is a vehicle for the intervention, not a contribution.

\paragraph{Invariance under label shift.}
When class priors differ between domains, matching marginal feature distributions cannot
also keep the joint error small~\cite{zhao2019invariant}; importance-weighted alignment
(IWDAN, IWCDAN) corrects for this with estimated target class priors~\cite{tachet2020domain},
which can be estimated without target labels by black-box shift estimation~\cite{lipton2018bbse}
or EM~\cite{saerens2002}. These results concern classification error. We study what the same
misalignment does to OOD monitoring, and show that a standard domain-leakage probe
misreports invariance under label shift.

\paragraph{OOD detection after adaptation.}
Full-spectrum OOD detection~\cite{yang2023,zhang2023} asks that covariate-shifted
in-distribution inputs be accepted; our smartphone and clinical images fall in that cell,
and Section~\ref{sec:tolerance} argues that acceptance is safe only when the model also
classifies them. Calibration degrades under shift~\cite{ovadia2019} and after domain
adaptation~\cite{wang2020}. Closest to our observation, Wu et al.\ report Mahalanobis and
ODIN AUROC below $0.5$ after adaptation, with rotated MNIST as in-distribution and rotated
notMNIST as OOD, with an MMD-based method
(DAN)~\cite{wu2023meta}, as a baseline in a study of continually adaptive detection. We
show the inversion on medical images, show that label shift drives it in our setting, and characterise
the mechanism.

\section{Materials and methods}

\subsection{Data and splits}

\textbf{ISIC~2019}~\cite{isic2019} (25{,}331 dermoscopic images, eight classes: MEL, NV,
BCC, AK, BKL, DF, VASC, SCC) is the in-distribution source. We split it $64/16/20$ into
train, validation and test \emph{grouped by} \texttt{lesion\_id} ($20\%$ held out for test, then $20\%$ of the remainder for validation; 16{,}211 / 4{,}053 /
5{,}067 images), because $60\%$ of test images in the image-level split share a lesion with
training~\cite{leakagepaper}. \textbf{PAD-UFES-20}~\cite{pacheco2020} (2{,}298 smartphone
photographs, six of the eight classes) is the domain the adversary aligns against. Its
labels are never used in any loss. We split it by patient into \texttt{pad\_adv} (1{,}582
images, 961 patients), seen by the adversarial and context branches, and
\texttt{pad\_heldout} (716 images, 412 patients), never seen.

\textbf{Unseen domains.} Four clinical-photograph datasets enter no part of training:
Fitzpatrick17k~\cite{groh2021} (3{,}887 images obtained), MED-NODE~\cite{giotis2015mednode}
(170 images: 70 melanoma, 100 nevi), SD-198~\cite{sun2016sd198} (6{,}584 images, 198 conditions)
and SCIN~\cite{ward2024scin} (10{,}406 images, 5{,}033 cases; SCIN Data Use License). All four
are used for evaluation only and are not redistributed. SD-198 was obtained from the release
linked by its first author; it states no licence, and its images originate from
DermQuest~\cite{sun2016sd198}.
SCIN is mostly inflammatory conditions; only $0.6\%$ of its images map to the ISIC classes,
so we use it only as an OOD set for detection and report it separately; no label-based
analysis is done on SCIN. Images are cropped to a lesion-centred box and resized to
$224\times224$.

\subsection{Model and training}

A dual-encoder model with EfficientNet-B3 backbones produces a lesion latent
$z_\ell\in\mathbb{R}^{16}$ (batch-normalised, $z_\ell^{\mathrm{norm}}$) used for diagnosis
through a linear head, and a context latent $z_c\in\mathbb{R}^{64}$. The objective is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cls}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{ctx}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ctx}}
+\lam\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{adv}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{orth}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{orth}},
\end{equation}
with $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cls}}$ on ISIC only, $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ctx}}$ a domain
cross-entropy on $z_c$, $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{adv}}$ a domain cross-entropy on
$z_\ell^{\mathrm{norm}}$ behind a gradient reversal layer, and $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{orth}}$
a squared-cosine penalty between the latents. AdamW (learning rate $10^{-4}$; adversary
$\times30$, classifier $\times0.2$), 40 epochs, domain-balanced batches. We sweep
$\lam\in\{0,0.25,0.5,1,2,4,8\}$ with everything else fixed (seeds $\{42,52,62,72,82\}$ at $\lam\in\{0,2\}$,
$\{42,\ldots,46\}$ elsewhere). A second recipe (A4: $z_\ell\in\mathbb{R}^{64}$, classifier
$\times1$, class-weighted loss) serves as a robustness check.

\paragraph{Checkpoints.} We report the last checkpoint. Validation balanced accuracy (eight
classes, chance $0.125$) has a lesion-clustered bootstrap SE of $0.023$--$0.026$, and the
best-validation and last checkpoints differ by less than one SE, so best-validation selection
picks early epochs by noise. Best-validation values are given for comparison.

\paragraph{Competence.} In-distribution balanced accuracy at $\lam=0$ is $0.481$ (main
recipe, last checkpoint) and $0.520$ (A4), $0.04$--$0.08$ below a standard single-encoder ERM on the same
split ($0.558\pm0.022$, five seeds). Most of the gap to published ISIC numbers comes from the
honest split: separating lesions lowers balanced accuracy by about $0.20$, and on the
image-level split, where test images share lesions with training, the same model reaches
$0.69$ (Table~\ref{tab:imagelevel}, leaky split; Appendix~\ref{app:imagelevel})~\cite{leakagepaper}.

\subsection{Interventions on label shift}

\textbf{Class mix seen by the adversary.} Keeping the classification loss on all of ISIC,
we resample only the ISIC images fed to the adversarial branch so that their class mix is
ISIC's own ($100\%$, the default), halfway to PAD's ($50\%$), or PAD's ($0\%$, no label
shift between the aligned domains), at $\lam\in\{0.5,2\}$ with five seeds (three at $50\%$).

\textbf{Importance-weighted alignment.} IWDAN and IWCDAN~\cite{tachet2020domain} reweight
the source in the adversarial loss by target class priors. In the original method the
weights are estimated online during training from unlabelled target predictions; as
alternatives we use priors from $50$ labelled \texttt{pad\_adv} images (a few-label setting) and
the true \texttt{pad\_adv} priors (oracle, not deployable). Black-box shift
estimates~\cite{lipton2018bbse} on the final checkpoints serve only as a diagnostic, and an EM
estimator~\cite{saerens2002} failed as implemented and is not used. CDAN~\cite{long2018cdan} is a
class-conditional adversarial baseline.

\subsection{Measurements}

\paragraph{OOD detection.} Class-conditional Mahalanobis with shared
covariance~\cite{lee2018} (primary), $k$-NN~\cite{sun2022}, maximum cosine to a class
centroid, maximum softmax probability~\cite{hendrycks2017} and energy~\cite{liu2020}, all
fit on ISIC train. AUROC treats OOD as the positive class, so $0.5$ is chance and values
below $0.5$ mean OOD images score as more familiar than ID test images. The five scores
are functions of the same latent, so they are not independent evidence.

\paragraph{Inversion.} A cell is inverted when the $95\%$ cluster-bootstrap interval of the
seed-mean Mahalanobis AUROC, and the intervals of at least three seeds, lie below $0.5$
(resampling details in Appendix~\ref{app:measure}). Runs with in-distribution balanced
accuracy below $0.25$ are excluded as degraded and reported.

\paragraph{Domain leakage.} Leakage is the balanced accuracy (chance $0.5$) of a probe for the
source dataset on frozen $z_\ell^{\mathrm{norm}}$, read in three ways: the linear probe of
earlier work, scored against all of PAD; probes on held-out images only (linear, MLP,
$k$-NN); and class-balanced and within-class probes that remove the effect of class mix
(Appendix~\ref{app:measure}).

\paragraph{Calibration and diagnosis.} Expected calibration error (15 bins) on ISIC test and
on \texttt{pad\_heldout} (six shared classes), and zero-shot balanced accuracy on
\texttt{pad\_heldout}. Out-of-domain ECE is close to mean confidence minus accuracy here, so
these are views of one phenomenon.

\paragraph{The sweep.} At the last checkpoint, Mahalanobis AUROC on \texttt{pad\_heldout}
falls from $0.825$ at $\lam=0$ to $0.445$ at $\lam=0.25$ and stays between $0.381$ and $0.445$
at every larger weight, meeting the inversion criterion at all six non-zero weights; on
Fitzpatrick17k it falls from $0.712$ to $0.354$--$0.405$ (Table~\ref{tab:dial}, Figure~\ref{fig:dial}). At
$\lam=2$ the seed-mean interval is $[0.358, 0.404]$ on \texttt{pad\_heldout} and
$[0.392, 0.419]$ on Fitzpatrick17k. In-distribution balanced accuracy stays within
$0.472$--$0.484$ (paired $\lam=2$ minus $\lam=0$: $-0.009$, $95\%$ CI $[-0.026, +0.006]$) and
in-distribution ECE within $0.202$--$0.210$, while out-of-domain ECE on \texttt{pad\_heldout}
rises from $0.305$ to $0.724$--$0.762$.
All five scores fall below $0.5$ on \texttt{pad\_heldout} at $\lam=2$ (seed means: $k$-NN $0.400$,
cosine $0.396$, MSP $0.411$, energy $0.425$); at the best-validation checkpoint the standard
$k$-th-neighbour $k$-NN gives the same values as our mean-of-$k$ variant within $0.01$. Checkpoint selection is discussed in Section~\ref{sec:discussion-ckpt}.

\paragraph{Unseen domains.} The inversion is not confined to the domain the adversary was
trained against (Table~\ref{tab:unseen}). On three clinical datasets that entered no part of
training, Mahalanobis AUROC falls from $0.712$ to $0.405$ (Fitzpatrick17k), $0.559$ to $0.288$
(MED-NODE) and $0.708$ to $0.376$ (SD-198) at $\lam=2$, inverted in all five seeds and at every
$\lam\ge0.25$ on MED-NODE and SD-198. Clustering near-duplicate SD-198 images ($6{,}016$ clusters)
leaves every interval and verdict unchanged. On SCIN, whose images are mostly inflammatory
conditions outside the ISIC label space, detection falls from $0.723$ to $0.551$ without
inverting.

\paragraph{Second backbone.} With DINOv2-S/14 in place of EfficientNet-B3 (dual encoder, five
seeds), Mahalanobis AUROC on \texttt{pad\_heldout} falls from $0.831\pm0.029$ at $\lam=0$ to
$0.448\pm0.045$ at $\lam=2$, with leakage $0.576\pm0.034$; Fitzpatrick17k falls from $0.778\pm0.016$
to $0.472\pm0.067$, and the inversion criterion is met on \texttt{pad\_heldout} and on all three
labelled unseen datasets (Appendix~\ref{app:dino}). This run does not rule out low competence as an
explanation: at $\lam=0$ the dual model reaches $0.470\pm0.015$ balanced accuracy against $0.565\pm0.015$
for ERM with the same backbone, a gap of $0.095$, above our $0.03$ margin. Selecting the backbone
learning rate on validation did not close the gap (Appendix~\ref{app:limits}). ERM with DINOv2 is
already below $0.5$ on MED-NODE ($0.452\pm0.033$), so MED-NODE is the weakest of the three.

\paragraph{Other splits and recipes.} On three further lesion-disjoint splits of ISIC,
Mahalanobis AUROC on \texttt{pad\_heldout} falls from $0.82$--$0.83$ at $\lam=0$ to
$0.39$--$0.43$ at $\lam=2$, and the unseen datasets fall below $0.5$ as well
(Appendix~\ref{app:splits}). A second recipe (A4) also inverts at $\lam=0.5$ and $\lam=2$
(Table~\ref{tab:unseen}), while a plain single-encoder DANN collapses to chance
without inverting ($0.51$--$0.56$ on the image-level split, best-validation checkpoint;
Appendix~\ref{app:single}; see also Appendix~\ref{app:limits}).

\subsection{Label shift between the aligned domains drives the inversion in this setting}
\label{sec:site}

\paragraph{Intervention.} Changing only the class mix of the ISIC images the adversary sees
switches the inversion on unseen domains on and off (Table~\ref{tab:shift}): paired by seed,
removing the label shift raises AUROC at $\lam=2$ by $0.361$ (Fitzpatrick17k), $0.243$
(MED-NODE) and $0.372$ (SD-198); per-level results are in Appendix~\ref{app:shift}.

\paragraph{Matched leakage.} Removing label shift also leaves more domain information in the
latent (leakage $0.791$ vs $0.634$), but a regression of unseen-domain AUROC on label-shift
level and leakage keeps a label-shift coefficient of $-0.107$ to $-0.172$ at fixed leakage on
all three unseen datasets (Appendix~\ref{app:shift}).

\paragraph{A natural example without DANN.} Label shift alone can invert a class-conditional
Mahalanobis score: training on BCN and treating the more nevus-heavy HAM as OOD gives AUROC
$0.369$ at $\lam=0$, and reweighting HAM to BCN's class mix brings it to $0.489$ (post hoc;
Appendix~\ref{app:site}). In the main setting the OOD sets are \emph{less} nevus-heavy than
ISIC, so detection works at $\lam=0$, and DANN creates the condition by moving them toward
nevus (Figure~\ref{fig:mech}a).

\paragraph{Other alignment objectives.} The inversion is not specific to gradient reversal.
Replacing the adversary with a multi-kernel MMD penalty~\cite{long2015} on the same model, at
held-out leakage matched to gradient reversal ($0.506$ vs $0.502$) and with in-distribution
accuracy within $0.04$, inverts detection on \texttt{pad\_heldout} ($0.354\pm0.022$; all five
scores $0.34$--$0.43$), as \citet{wu2023meta} observed with rotated MNIST and notMNIST. On the unseen datasets it only collapses
(Fitzpatrick17k $0.553$, MED-NODE $0.523$, SD-198 $0.535$). CORAL alignment at the same leakage
leaves detection intact ($0.820$; $0.788$, $0.702$, $0.788$). Matching the full feature distribution, adversarially or by
kernel MMD, is what misaligns classes under label shift; matching second moments is not
enough.

\subsection{Mechanism: misaligned classes behind chance-level leakage}
\label{sec:mechanism}
\label{sec:nonmediation}

\paragraph{Unfamiliar images move to the majority class.} At $\lam=2$ with full label shift (Figure~\ref{fig:mech}a),
the share of \texttt{pad\_heldout} images whose nearest class centroid is nevus rises from
$0.36$ to $0.63$, and to $0.75$--$0.85$ on the unseen sets; without label shift it stays at
$0.12$ and $0.39$--$0.49$. Images of non-nevus classes are pulled into the nevus region:
split by true class, non-nevus OOD images fall to AUROC $0.42$ (main recipe) and $0.47$ (A4),
whereas images still nearest a non-nevus centroid stay at $0.62$ and $0.64$. Let $\mathrm{AUROC}_c$ compare all OOD images with the ID test images of
class $c$. Because AUROC averages over ID samples, the pooled AUROC equals
$\sum_c \pi_c\,\mathrm{AUROC}_c$, with $\pi_c$ the share of class $c$ in the ID test set (verified
numerically to $10^{-15}$). Nevus is half of the ISIC test set, so OOD images placed in the nevus
region are enough to push the pooled score below chance.

\paragraph{The displacement follows the domain direction.} For each OOD image we measured
how far it lies along the direction separating ISIC from PAD in the $\lam=0$ model, and how
much closer to its nearest ID centroid it moves at $\lam=2$. The two correlate on every
lesion dataset (Spearman $0.33$--$0.47$), while the deviation orthogonal to that direction
does not predict the pull ($|\rho|\le0.07$ on four of five sets). Between two $\lam=0$ models
of different seeds the same correlation is smaller ($0.11$--$0.29$); the DANN-specific
excess exceeds $0.15$ on all four unseen datasets ($0.18$--$0.33$) but not on
\texttt{pad\_heldout} ($0.057$), and is larger in the pre-projection backbone ($0.24$--$0.45$
on all five).

\paragraph{Chance-level leakage hides the misalignment.} The domain probe of
Table~\ref{tab:shift} (as in earlier work) is scored partly on images the adversary trained on,
which is why it reads $0.928$ at $\lam=0$ against $0.842$ for the held-out probe of Table~\ref{tab:dial}. On held-out images
a linear probe reads the domain at about chance from $\lam=0.25$ on ($0.502$--$0.527$ at the
last checkpoint, Table~\ref{tab:dial}), but a $k$-NN probe still reads it ($0.55$--$0.61$ from
$\lam=0.5$ on; best-validation checkpoint, Appendix~\ref{app:probes}), and under label shift an unweighted probe misreports
invariance (Table~\ref{tab:probes}). With full label shift DANN reaches $0.510$ on an
unweighted held-out probe, yet within each class the domain remains decodable ($0.618$),
more so than in any other condition. Correcting the class mix the adversary aligns (shift
$0\%$, or importance weighting with the true priors) raises the unweighted reading
($0.63$--$0.64$) while lowering the within-class one ($0.56$--$0.57$). Under label shift,
DANN matches the marginal distributions by misaligning classes, as the theory of
\citet{zhao2019invariant} implies; leakage should therefore be measured within class
(post hoc).

\subsection{Safety-relevant misclassification and mitigation}
\label{sec:confidently}
\label{sec:adversarial}

\paragraph{Melanomas called benign.} With a threshold accepting $95\%$ of ISIC validation
images, $0.90$--$1.00$ of the malignant images from external datasets are accepted at both
$\lam=0$ and $\lam=2$, so the monitor does not catch them in either case. What changes is the
classifier: the share of melanomas predicted nevus with confidence above $0.9$ rises from
$12.5\%$ $[7.5, 17.6]$ to $51.1\%$ $[41.4, 61.0]$ on Fitzpatrick17k ($n=83$) and from $12.9\%$
$[8.8, 17.2]$ to $81.4\%$ $[75.9, 86.5]$ on SD-198 ($n=113$) between $\lam=0$ and $\lam=2$
(Appendix~\ref{app:clinical}); BCC, SCC and AK follow the same pattern ($50$--$87\%$ at
$\lam=2$). Removing the label shift seen by the adversary (shift $0\%$) lowers the melanoma
share at $\lam=2$ from $53.0\%$ to $21.4\%$ on Fitzpatrick17k and from $83.2\%$ to $33.8\%$
on SD-198 (retrained runs of Table~\ref{tab:shift}).
For \texttt{pad\_heldout} as a whole, the Mahalanobis monitor already accepts $91.1\%$ of images as
in-distribution at $\lam=0$; at $\lam=2$ it accepts $96.1\%$ (Appendix~\ref{app:clinical}).

\paragraph{No reliable scoring-side fix among the variants tested.} Reversing the sign of the
score is not a remedy: at
$\lam\ge0.5$ it reaches only $0.55$--$0.63$ on \texttt{pad\_heldout} and Fitzpatrick17k with a
false-positive rate of $0.84$--$0.98$ at $95\%$ true-positive rate across datasets, and at $\lam=0$ it would
turn $0.808$ into $0.192$. Relative Mahalanobis~\cite{ren2021relative} and per-class
standardised distances stay below $0.5$ on all three unseen datasets at $\lam=2$. Reversing the score would also require knowing in advance
that the model is inverted. Among the variants we tried, only changes during training,
through the class priors used by the adversary, improved detection.

\paragraph{Importance-weighted alignment.} With target class priors estimated online without
labels, as in the original IWDAN, the estimate stays about $0.5$ total variation from the truth
and detection recovers only in part ($32$--$45\%$ of the lost AUROC at $\lam=2$, $66$--$76\%$
at $\lam=0.5$; MED-NODE stays inverted). With priors from $50$ labelled target images,
detection on the unseen domains returns to the level obtained with the true priors, but
in-distribution balanced accuracy drops from $0.48$ to $0.39$--$0.40$ (post hoc). CDAN and
IWCDAN stay inverted at $\lam=2$; details are in Appendix~\ref{app:prior}.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{Accepting shifted inputs.}\label{sec:tolerance}
Full-spectrum OOD detection argues that covariate-shifted inputs should be
accepted~\cite{yang2023}. After DANN, our models accept images from external sources more
readily than their own test images, but their zero-shot balanced accuracy on these images
is only $0.244$ at $\lam=2$ (chance $0.167$). AUROC below $0.5$ means that the ranking is
reversed: the monitor scores the shifted images as more typical than real in-distribution
data. Whether a monitor tolerates shifted inputs is a separate question, answered by the
acceptance rate at the deployed threshold ($0.90$--$1.00$ here, Appendix~\ref{app:clinical})
together with the accuracy on the same images. We think claims of tolerance to covariate shift should be
reported together with the accuracy on the same inputs.

\paragraph{Why label shift matters.}
\citet{zhao2019invariant} showed that when class priors differ, matching marginal feature
distributions cannot keep the classes of both domains in place. Our results show the effect
of this on a distance-based monitor. OOD images that differ from the source along the domain
direction are moved into the region of the majority class, and a class-conditional score
then treats them as typical. The BCN$\to$HAM case shows that an OOD set with more nevi than
the ID set can invert such a score even without DANN. In the main setting the OOD sets have
fewer nevi than ISIC, and DANN creates the problem by moving them toward nevus. Without label
shift the inversion is only seen on the adversary's target domain, where a smaller
within-class pull remains. This misplacement is also why the unweighted domain probe reaches
chance level, since it measures marginal rather than class-conditional alignment. Our
experiments do not establish why a plain single-encoder DANN collapses detection to chance
instead of inverting it. Our hypothesis is that it never reaches the depth of invariance at
which classes are misaligned: even with a stronger adversary its held-out leakage stayed at
$0.70$ (Appendix~\ref{app:limits}).

\paragraph{Checkpoint selection.}\label{sec:discussion-ckpt}
We noticed after the sweep that at $\lam=0.25$ the last checkpoint is inverted on both
\texttt{pad\_heldout} and Fitzpatrick17k, while best-validation selection picks earlier
epochs where the seeds still disagree (seed s.d.\ $0.121$). Validation accuracy does not
separate these checkpoints, so a model selected in the usual way may look safe only because
training was stopped early.



\medskip
\noindent\fbox{\begin{minipage}{\dimexpr\textwidth-2\fboxsep-2\fboxrule\relax}
\textbf{Recommendations.} After a domain-alignment step:
\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
\item[(i)] check OOD detection again on images from unseen sources, per class and pooled;
\item[(ii)] report domain leakage within class and not only unweighted;
\item[(iii)] compare the class priors of the aligned domains and correct for them if they differ: online IWDAN without labels recovered $66$--$76\%$ of the lost AUROC on \texttt{pad\_heldout} and Fitzpatrick17k at $\lam=0.5$ without loss of in-distribution accuracy (MED-NODE stayed inverted), and a small labelled sample from the target domain brought detection to the true-prior level in a post hoc analysis;
\item[(iv)] report results for more than one checkpoint.
\end{itemize}
\end{minipage}}

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
\item Scope: dermatology only; the spread to unseen domains is shown for gradient reversal.
\item Other modalities: could not be tested; DANN did not remove hospital information on Camelyon17 while preserving the classifier, and the chest-radiograph OOD set was near chance before adaptation ($0.522$).
\item Architecture: single-encoder DANN did not reach invariance with EfficientNet-B3 or DINOv2-S/14, so the inversion is untested there.
\item Competence: in-distribution accuracy is $0.04$--$0.08$ below ERM on the same split ($0.095$ for DINOv2).
\item Statistics: MED-NODE and SD-198 are resampled by image; the five scores share one latent.
\item Mechanism: a Gaussian toy model with label shift did not reproduce the inversion.
\item Warning signals: none computed on in-distribution data alone flagged inverted runs.
\item Mitigation: the few-label remedy is post hoc and costs in-distribution accuracy.
\item Data: we obtained $3{,}887$ of $16{,}577$ Fitzpatrick17k images; the inversion holds within each skin-type group.
\end{itemize}
Each point is given in full in Appendix~\ref{app:limits}.

\section{Conclusion}
We showed that domain-adversarial training can make an OOD monitor rank held-out images from the aligned domain and from external sources as more
in-distribution than the model's own test images. In our experiments (one dermatology task, a dual-encoder model with EfficientNet-B3 and DINOv2-S/14 backbones), this is driven mainly by label shift between the
domains that the adversary aligns. The adversary moves unfamiliar images toward the majority
class, and the usual leakage probe does not detect this misalignment. Checking OOD detection
on unseen sources after adaptation, measuring leakage within class and correcting for class
priors are cheap steps. With a small labelled sample from the target domain, the last of
these also reduced the problem in a post hoc analysis.

remains without label shift ($0.458$; paired difference $+0.093$). One of five shift-$0\%$
runs collapsed (in-distribution balanced accuracy $0.139$) and is excluded.

\paragraph{Matched leakage.} Removing label shift also leaves more domain information in the
latent (leakage $0.791$ vs $0.634$), so the intervention alone does not separate label shift
from depth of invariance. A regression of unseen-domain AUROC on label-shift level and
leakage, with recipe as a covariate (variance inflation $\approx1.04$), gives label-shift
coefficients of $-0.166$ $[-0.217, -0.115]$ (Fitzpatrick17k), $-0.107$ $[-0.148, -0.065]$
(MED-NODE) and $-0.172$ $[-0.221, -0.123]$ (SD-198) for a change from no shift to full shift
at fixed leakage (shift coded $0$, $0.5$, $1$; wild cluster bootstrap with Webb weights over
recipe $\times$ $\lam$ $\times$ shift cells, $2{,}000$ draws). On \texttt{pad\_heldout}
the coefficient is $+0.041$ $[-0.033, +0.110]$. Comparing runs at equal leakage across recipes
(A4 at shift $100\%$, leakage $0.78$, against the main recipe at shift $0\%$, leakage $0.79$)
gives the same direction ($+0.23$ to $+0.26$).

by estimated target class priors, and how well it works depends on the estimate.
With weights updated online from unlabelled target predictions, as in the original method,
the implied target priors end $0.43$--$0.55$ (total variation; mean $0.515$) from the truth: nevus is
down-weighted from $0.51$ toward $0.23$--$0.28$, against a true $0.10$. IWDAN then keeps leakage at the DANN level ($0.642$ vs $0.634$) and recovers $32\%$ of the lost AUROC on
\texttt{pad\_heldout} ($0.513$) and $45\%$ on Fitzpatrick17k ($0.508$), while MED-NODE
($0.350$) and SD-198 ($0.488$) stay at or below chance. At $\lam=0.5$ the online priors are closer to the truth (total variation $0.38$--$0.47$,
mean $0.428$) and the same method recovers more: $0.667$ on \texttt{pad\_heldout} and $0.633$ on Fitzpatrick17k ($66\%$ and $76\%$ of
the gap), at leakage $0.665$ against $0.628$ for DANN, and SD-198 recovers to $0.607$ ($71\%$);
MED-NODE improves from $0.310$ to $0.447$ but stays inverted. Unlike the variants with known
priors, this costs no in-distribution accuracy ($0.482$ against $0.483$), and the within-class
probe is lower than under DANN ($0.588$ against $0.618$). \emph{EM} estimation~\cite{saerens2002} failed as implemented, including after a bug fix:
in two of five seeds it placed mass on DF and VASC, which are absent from PAD, so its results
are not used in any comparison. With priors from \emph{$50$ labelled target images} (total variation
$0.10$--$0.14$), detection on the unseen domains returns to the level obtained with the true
priors (Fitzpatrick17k $0.741$ vs $0.717$, SD-198 $0.734$ vs $0.693$, MED-NODE $0.606$ vs $0.563$;
\texttt{pad\_heldout} $0.619$ vs $0.647$), and within-class leakage is lower than under DANN
($0.58$ vs $0.62$). This is a promising but post hoc mitigation: neither the 50-label nor the true-prior
variant meets our leakage criterion, because unweighted leakage rises ($0.81$ and $0.78$
against $0.63$), and both cost in-distribution balanced accuracy ($0.39$--$0.40$, against
$0.48$ both under DANN and before adaptation). Read descriptively, detection improves as the prior error falls ($0.52$:
partial; $0.10$--$0.14$: close to the true-prior level; $0$: true priors), but a rank-correlation test
across estimators did not support this (Spearman $-0.33$, $[-0.80, +0.28]$), with EM
contributing invalid priors. As a diagnostic, black-box shift estimates computed on the final checkpoints were further
from the truth ($0.65$--$0.76$): their assumption of unchanged class-conditional predictions
fails across these domains, with target BCC and AK images predicted as MEL or NV. Label-free
prior estimates thus remain about $0.5$ total variation off and recover detection only in part;
$50$ labels bring the error to $0.10$--$0.14$ and detection close to the true-prior level.

\section{Sign reversal and scoring-side variants}
\label{app:flip}

Reversing the Mahalanobis score with the direction chosen on \texttt{pad\_heldout} at $\lam=2$
gives $0.192$ at $\lam=0$ and $0.546$--$0.625$ on \texttt{pad\_heldout} and Fitzpatrick17k at
$\lam\ge0.5$; on MED-NODE it reaches $0.69$--$0.71$; false-positive rates at $95\%$ true-positive rate stay

Mahalanobis at $\lam=2$: Fitzpatrick17k $0.443$, MED-NODE $0.376$, SD-198 $0.427$,
\texttt{pad\_heldout} $0.439$; per-class standardised Mahalanobis: $0.478$, $0.340$, $0.447$,
$0.424$.

\paragraph{Discussion.} Reversing the score only helps if one already knows that the model is inverted. At $\lam=0$
it would break a working monitor, and at $\lam\ge0.5$ most OOD images are still accepted at a
$95\%$ ID-acceptance threshold. Relative Mahalanobis, which is less sensitive to class
structure, also stays below chance. Among the variants we tried, only changes during training,
through the class priors used by the adversary, improved detection.

\FloatBarrier
\section{Negative results}
\label{app:negative}

\textbf{Warning signals.} Thresholds set at the $95$th percentile of each statistic over
$\lam=0$ runs. A centroid-collapse statistic computed on in-distribution data only did not
track inversion (sensitivity $0.15$--$0.38$; Spearman with \texttt{pad\_heldout} AUROC $0.03$
and $-0.14$ for the validation and test versions, over $64$ runs). The share
of unlabelled deployment images predicted as the majority class tracked it (Spearman $-0.77$ $[-0.85, -0.63]$ against
\texttt{pad\_heldout} AUROC and $-0.75$ $[-0.85, -0.57]$ against Fitzpatrick17k AUROC over $64$ runs; sensitivity $1.00$, specificity $0.75$ on twelve non-inverting runs with
$\lam>0$ under the Fitzpatrick17k label, $0.33$ on six under the \texttt{pad\_heldout} label).
\textbf{Toy model.} Two-domain Gaussian mixtures with a majority class at $50\%$ in the source
and a label-shift level from $0$ to $1$, with a small encoder and gradient reversal, produced
no inversion at any setting (Mahalanobis AUROC $0.57$--$0.66$); leakage in the toy stayed
near $0.5$--$0.58$, so it may not reproduce the conditions of the real data.

\FloatBarrier
\section{Experiments that did not take place}
\label{app:external}

\textbf{Camelyon17 by hospital.} In the domain-adversarial runs with hospital-disjoint
held-out slides, the selected checkpoints were all from the warm-up epoch before gradient
reversal took effect (15 of 15 runs), so these runs do not test the hypothesis. In runs
whose selected checkpoint followed the warm-up (epochs 6--10), the hospital probe fell by at
most $0.044$ with in-distribution accuracy $\ge0.99$, and at $\lam=300$ the probe fell by
$0.081$ while out-of-domain accuracy dropped from $0.91$ to $0.70$. In no setting did DANN
remove hospital information while preserving the classifier. \textbf{Chest radiographs.} With
NIH ChestX-ray14 and pediatric radiographs as the aligned domains, Mahalanobis AUROC on
Shenzhen was $0.522$ before any adaptation, below our headroom requirement, so we stopped
before training adversarial models.

\section{Measurement details}
\label{app:measure}

\paragraph{Inversion.} We call a cell inverted when the cluster-bootstrap $95\%$ CI of the
seed-mean Mahalanobis AUROC lies below $0.5$ and at least three seeds' own intervals do
(all three in cells with three seeds). Bootstrap draws ($2{,}000$) resample ISIC test by lesion and OOD sets by
patient (\texttt{pad\_heldout}), near-duplicate cluster (Fitzpatrick17k) or case (SCIN);
MED-NODE and SD-198 carry no patient identifiers and are resampled by image, so their
intervals may be too narrow. Runs with in-distribution
balanced accuracy below $0.25$ are excluded as degraded and reported.

\paragraph{Domain leakage.} Balanced accuracy (chance $0.5$) of a logistic probe for the
source dataset on frozen $z_\ell^{\mathrm{norm}}$. The probe used in earlier work scores
ISIC test against all of PAD, including images the adversary trained on. We therefore also
report probes on held-out images only (linear, one-hidden-layer MLP, $k$-NN), a
class-balanced probe that reweights both domains to a common class mix, and a within-class
probe averaged over classes.

The best-validation checkpoint gives the same picture as the last checkpoint from $\lam=0.5$
on, but at $\lam=0.25$ it selects an earlier epoch and the inversion is not yet visible
($0.525$, seed s.d.\ $0.121$; an observation made after the sweep).

\FloatBarrier
\section{Limitations in detail}
\label{app:limits}

\textbf{Scope.} The inversion is shown in dermatology only, and its spread to unseen domains
is shown for gradient reversal; multi-kernel MMD inverts detection on the target domain but
only collapses it on the unseen datasets. We attempted two other
modalities and neither experiment took place: on Camelyon17 no adversarial setting we tried
removed hospital information while preserving the classifier, and on chest radiographs the
detector was near chance on the OOD set before any adaptation (AUROC $0.522$), so there was
nothing to invert. \textbf{Architecture.} The main results use a dual-encoder model; the A4
recipe changes the latent size and loss, but a plain single-encoder DANN collapses detection
to chance without inverting, and whether deeper invariance would invert it is untested: with a stronger adversary
(warm-up, higher adversary learning rate, deeper head) its held-out leakage stayed at
$0.70$, above our target of $0.55$, and detection stayed low without inverting ($0.52$ on
\texttt{pad\_heldout}, $0.62$ on Fitzpatrick17k). Without label shift, raising $\lam$ to $8$ brought held-out leakage to chance in only one
of five runs; in that run detection on the unseen datasets did not invert, which is consistent
with label shift being necessary but rests on a single model. A single-encoder DANN with DINOv2-S/14 at $\lam=2$ (five seeds) also stayed above our invariance
threshold of $0.60$ (leakage $0.884\pm0.019$; ID $0.555\pm0.023$, \texttt{pad\_heldout}
$0.546\pm0.033$, Fitzpatrick17k $0.476\pm0.069$), so the test did not take place for this model either (Table~\ref{tab:single-dino}).
\textbf{Competence.} In-distribution balanced accuracy is $0.04$--$0.08$
below a standard ERM on the same lesion-disjoint split, and $0.095$ below ERM for the DINOv2-S/14 model.
To check whether the backbone learning rate caused this gap, we selected it on validation (seed $42$):
validation balanced accuracy at $\times0.1$, $\times0.3$ and $\times1.0$ was $0.444$, $0.402$ and $0.236$
for the dual model at $\lam=0$ and $0.516$, $0.504$ and $0.418$ for ERM. Both chose $\times0.1$, the setting
already used, so the gap is not due to the learning rate. \textbf{Statistics.} MED-NODE and
SD-198 carry no patient identifiers and are resampled by image, so their intervals may be too
narrow; SCIN is used only as an OOD set because almost none of it maps to the ISIC classes.
The five OOD scores are functions of one latent and are not independent evidence.
\textbf{Mechanism.} A two-domain Gaussian toy model with label shift did not reproduce the
inversion, so the conditions we identify may not be sufficient in general. \textbf{Warning
signals.} No statistic computed on in-distribution data alone flagged the inverted runs; the
share of deployment images predicted as the majority class tracked inversion
(Spearman $-0.77$ and $-0.75$ over $64$ runs) but needs unlabelled target data. \textbf{Mitigation.} The few-label
remedy is a post hoc analysis, costs in-distribution accuracy, and did not meet our leakage
criterion. \textbf{Data.} We obtained $3{,}887$ of $16{,}577$ Fitzpatrick17k images. The host refused
the remaining downloads (HTTP 406), so we cannot tell removed images from an unavailable host, and the
subset differs from the full set by Fitzpatrick type (total variation $0.257$; $0.063$ by ISIC-mapped
class). As a robustness check, Mahalanobis AUROC by skin type (types 1--2, 3--4 and 5--6; $n=819$,
$2{,}078$, $837$) is $0.733$, $0.711$ and $0.695$ at $\lam=0$ and $0.423$, $0.390$ and $0.411$ at $\lam=2$;
reweighting to the full type mix gives $0.719$ and $0.415$, and reweighting by type and class gives
$0.714$ and $0.424$. At $\lam=2$ all three groups and both reweighted versions meet the inversion
criterion; the $153$ images of unknown type ($0.479$) do not.
