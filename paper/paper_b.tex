\begin{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Background.} Domain-adversarial invariance training is widely
recommended for removing acquisition shortcuts from medical imaging models.
Its effect on the out-of-distribution (OOD) monitoring that such models rely
on at deployment has not been measured.

\noindent\textbf{Methods.} We swept the adversarial weight $\lam$ across seven
values with three to five seeds each (27 runs) on ISIC~2019 (dermoscopy,
25{,}331 images) and PAD-UFES-20 (clinical photographs, 2{,}298 images),
holding everything else fixed. At every $\lam$ we measured domain leakage by
linear probe, in-distribution balanced accuracy, calibration on in-distribution
and out-of-domain data, OOD detection with five scoring rules, and zero-shot
cross-domain diagnostic accuracy on a patient-level held-out PAD partition. We
repeated the sweep with plain single-encoder adversarial training and with four
non-adversarial invariance objectives, and validated on Fitzpatrick17k, a third
dataset never seen by the adversary.

\noindent\textbf{Results.} At the smallest non-zero weight tested, OOD detection
fell from AUROC $0.843$ to $0.513$; by $\lam = 2$ it had \textbf{inverted below
chance to $0.427$} ($95\%$ CI $[0.414, 0.439]$; all five seeds individually below
$0.5$), ranking images from a clinic the model had never seen as
more in-distribution than its own test data, throughout the score distribution
rather than only in the tails. All five scoring rules collapsed together, the
inversion held on the third dataset ($0.391$ at $\lam = 2$ against $0.764$ at
$\lam = 0$, the same model and data) and survived shrinkage estimation. The collapse was a
cliff rather than a trade-off: no operating point combined invariance with
detection. It was invisible to routine monitoring --- domain leakage fell to its
chance floor ($0.915 \to 0.553$, floor $0.5$) and in-distribution balanced
accuracy did not degrade ($0.692 \to 0.707$). Confidence on never-seen-domain
images rose from $0.489$ to $0.930$, overtaking in-distribution confidence,
while cross-domain accuracy did not improve at any $\lam$ and predictions
collapsed toward the benign majority class (actinic keratosis recall
$0.244 \to 0.016$, $n = \nAK$). The failure reproduced in plain single-encoder
gradient-reversal training but not in moment-matching objectives, which reduced
leakage comparably without collapsing any detector. In a same-modality shift between
two hospitals the collapse reproduced ($k$-NN $0.658 \to 0.486$) but the inversion did
not: with matched baseline detectability, Mahalanobis fell only to $0.683$. Six
candidate explanations were tested and
refuted, and a pre-registered synthetic reproduction failed.

\noindent\textbf{Conclusions.} Across imaging modality, domain-adversarial training
does not merely weaken out-of-distribution monitoring; it reverses it, while every
routinely monitored metric improves. Between two hospitals sharing a modality the
collapse persists and the reversal does not, so the two should be claimed separately. OOD detection and out-of-domain calibration should
be reported as a function of achieved invariance rather than at a single
operating point.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} domain-adversarial training; out-of-distribution
detection; calibration; distribution shift; skin lesion classification;
model monitoring

\section{Introduction}

Deep models for skin lesion classification are known to exploit acquisition
artefacts rather than pathology: ruler marks, ink annotations, imaging
hardware and site identity all correlate with diagnosis in public benchmarks
\cite{winkler2019,bissoto2020,daneshjou2022}. The standard remedy is to make
the representation invariant to the acquisition domain, most often through
domain-adversarial training with a gradient reversal layer
\cite{ganin2016,bousmalis2016}. The stated purpose of this remedy is safe
deployment across sites.

Deployment across sites also relies on a second mechanism: out-of-distribution
monitoring. A deployed system is expected to flag inputs unlike its training
data so that a human reviews them rather than the model acting alone. This
paper asks what invariance training does to that mechanism.

The answer is that it reverses it. Sweeping the adversarial weight in a
controlled setting, we find that out-of-distribution detection does not merely
weaken as domain information is removed --- it passes through chance and
inverts. At the weights that achieve invariance, a model asked which inputs are
unlike its training data answers with its own test split, and ranks images from
a clinic it has never seen as the more routine of the two. AUROC falls from
$0.843$ to $0.427$, with a $95\%$ confidence interval of $[0.414, 0.439]$ that lies
entirely below chance, across every scoring rule we tried.

Degradation would be unsurprising. A representation stripped of domain
information should be worse at recognising a domain, and chance is what that
argument predicts. Inversion is not predicted by it, and after testing and
refuting six explanations and one pre-registered synthetic reproduction, we
cannot account for it.

What makes the reversal consequential rather than merely odd is that nothing in
a standard evaluation reveals it. Over the same range the model becomes more
confident on unseen-domain images than on its own held-out test data, and its
predictions collapse toward the benign majority class. Meanwhile
in-distribution accuracy does not fall, in-distribution calibration does not
change, and the domain leakage probe --- the very metric the technique is
adopted to improve --- improves exactly as intended.

The technique is adopted for safe deployment across sites, and it disables the
cross-site safety machinery while every monitored number moves in the direction
its user is hoping for.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A controlled intervention: a seven-point sweep of the adversarial weight,
27 training runs with everything else held fixed, isolating invariance pressure
from architectural and data confounds.
\item The inversion: OOD detection passes through chance and reverses, as a
cliff rather than a trade-off curve, across five scoring rules, on a third
dataset the adversary never saw, and robustly to shrinkage estimation and tail
truncation.
\item The invisibility: in-distribution accuracy, in-distribution calibration
and the domain leakage probe all fail to reveal it, and the leakage probe does
not mediate it, so it cannot serve as a proxy.
\item Scope: the failure reproduces in plain single-encoder adversarial
training, so it is not an artefact of the factorized architecture; and it does
not appear under moment-matching objectives that reduce leakage along the same
axis, so it belongs to adversarial optimisation rather than to invariance.
\item Six candidate explanations tested and refuted, plus a pre-registered
synthetic reproduction that failed --- delimiting the phenomenon for whoever
explains it.
\item A reporting protocol that makes the failure visible at near-zero cost.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Shortcut learning in dermatology.}
Winkler et al.~\cite{winkler2019} showed that ink markings shift melanoma
predictions; Bissoto et al.~\cite{bissoto2020} showed that classifiers retain
competitive accuracy on ISIC data when the lesion itself is masked;
Daneshjou et al.~\cite{daneshjou2022} documented degraded performance on
darker skin tones. These motivate invariance-based remedies.

\paragraph{Invariant representation learning.}
Domain-adversarial training \cite{ganin2016} and domain separation networks
\cite{bousmalis2016} remove domain information by adversarial or
orthogonality constraints. \textbf{The architecture used here is
equivalent to a domain separation network and is not a contribution of this
paper.} It is a vehicle for the intervention.

\paragraph{Covariate shift versus semantic novelty.}
Full-spectrum OOD detection \cite{yang2023} argues that the OOD literature
conflates two distinct shifts, and proposes a four-way taxonomy: training ID,
\emph{covariate-shifted ID}, near-OOD and far-OOD. Crucially, it prescribes
that covariate-shifted in-distribution samples should be \textbf{tolerated as
in-distribution rather than flagged}, on the grounds that rejecting them
contradicts the goal of generalization. OpenOOD v1.5 \cite{zhang2023} adopts
the same full-spectrum setting at ImageNet scale and for foundation models.

Our setting falls squarely in the covariate-shifted-ID cell: PAD-UFES-20
contains the same diagnostic classes as ISIC, photographed differently. A
reader of \cite{yang2023} might therefore regard a model that accepts these
images as in-distribution as behaving \emph{correctly}.

We argue this is exactly the gap. \textbf{Tolerance of covariate shift is safe
only when accompanied by generalization, and the two are evaluated
separately or not at all.} Our models achieve the prescribed tolerance --- by
$\lam = 2$ they rank out-of-domain images as more in-distribution than their
own test data --- while cross-domain balanced accuracy sits at $0.249$ against
a floor of $0.167$, plain accuracy is below the majority-class rate, and
confidence on those images exceeds in-distribution confidence. Tolerating
inputs one cannot classify is worse than flagging them, and no current
protocol separates the two cases. We therefore report acceptance and
cross-domain accuracy jointly throughout.

\paragraph{Calibration under adaptation.}
Ovadia et al.~\cite{ovadia2019} showed that calibration degrades under shift for
ordinary training, and Wang et al.~\cite{wang2020} established the sharper result that
domain adaptation methods specifically \emph{trade} calibration for accuracy: adapted
models are miscalibrated on the target domain, and they propose a post-hoc correction.
\textbf{The calibration degradation we report in Section~\ref{sec:confidently} is
therefore not a new phenomenon.} What is new here is its dose-dependence on the
adversarial weight, its invisibility to in-distribution calibration, and --- separately
from calibration altogether --- the collapse and inversion of out-of-distribution
detection, which a post-hoc confidence correction does not address. \paragraph{Calibration as a route to invariance --- and the reverse.}
Wald et al.~\cite{wald2021} established the forward direction: enforcing \emph{multi-domain calibration}
yields invariance, with models so calibrated provably free of certain spurious
correlations, and they propose a calibration loss (CLOvE) validated on WILDS.

Our results show the reverse implication does not hold. Obtaining invariance
adversarially does not yield calibration; it \emph{destroys} out-of-domain
calibration in a dose-dependent way while leaving in-distribution calibration
untouched. Calibration and adversarial invariance are therefore not
interchangeable routes to the same representation, and the direction of
enforcement matters.

\paragraph{What is new here.}
(i) The \emph{shape} of the dependence --- a cliff at the smallest effective
adversarial weight rather than a graded trade-off; (ii) \emph{sub-chance}
inversion, in which out-of-domain data is ranked as more typical than
in-distribution data throughout the score distribution; (iii) the
demonstration that the failure is invisible to every routinely monitored
metric; (iv) the finding that the standard domain leakage probe neither
mediates nor predicts it; (v) the consequence for calibration and for
diagnostic decisions on out-of-domain data; and (vi) the observation that
full-spectrum tolerance is achieved here without the generalization that would
make it safe.

\section{Materials and methods}

\subsection{Data}

\textbf{ISIC 2019} \cite{isic2019}: 25{,}331 dermoscopic images, eight
diagnostic classes (MEL, NV, BCC, AK, BKL, DF, VASC, SCC), CC~BY-NC~4.0.
\textbf{PAD-UFES-20} \cite{pacheco2020}: 2{,}298 smartphone clinical
photographs, six classes mapping into the same label space (DF and VASC
absent), CC~BY~4.0. \textbf{Fitzpatrick17k} \cite{groh2021}: 3{,}887 of
16{,}577 images obtained; annotations CC~BY-NC-SA~3.0, images web-scraped
under mixed copyright, used for evaluation only and not redistributed.

Images were cropped to a soft lesion-centred bounding box via Otsu
thresholding on the deviation from the border median, expanded by a 0.30
margin with a 0.70 minimum crop ratio, and resized to $224\times224$.

\subsection{Splits and the PAD held-out set}

ISIC was split $60/20/20$ into train/validation/test, stratified by label
(\texttt{random\_state}~$=42$).

\textbf{PAD appears in training and would otherwise also be the OOD set.}
The classification loss never sees PAD labels (\texttt{ignore\_index}$=-1$),
but PAD images enter the context and adversarial branches. To remove the
resulting circularity we partitioned PAD \emph{at patient level} into
\texttt{pad\_adv} (1{,}582 images, 961 patients), visible to those branches
during training, and \texttt{pad\_heldout} (716 images, 412 patients), never
seen in any form. No patient and no lesion appears in both. \textbf{All OOD
and cross-domain numbers reported in the main text use
\texttt{pad\_heldout}.} An earlier draft of this work reported $0.413$ at $\lam = 2$;
that value was scored on \texttt{pad\_full}, which includes the $1{,}582$ images the
adversarial branch saw during training. Fitzpatrick17k provides a second never-adversarial
domain.

\subsection{Architecture and objective}

A dual-encoder model with EfficientNet-B3 backbones produces a lesion latent
$z_{\ell} \in \mathbb{R}^{16}$ (batch-normalised, denoted
$z_{\ell}^{\mathrm{norm}}$) and a context latent $z_{c} \in \mathbb{R}^{64}$.
Diagnosis uses $z_{\ell}^{\mathrm{norm}}$ only, through a linear head. The
objective is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cls}}
+ \lambda_{\mathrm{ctx}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ctx}}
+ \lam\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{adv}}
+ \lambda_{\mathrm{orth}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{orth}},
\end{equation}
with $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cls}}$ on ISIC only, $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ctx}}$ a
domain cross-entropy on $z_c$, $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{adv}}$ a domain
cross-entropy on $z_{\ell}^{\mathrm{norm}}$ behind a gradient reversal layer,
and $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{orth}}$ a squared-cosine penalty between the two
latents ($\lambda_{\mathrm{orth}} = 1$ throughout).

AdamW, base learning rate $10^{-4}$, adversary learning rate multiplier $30$,
classifier multiplier $0.2$, 40 epochs, batches balanced across domains,
checkpoint selected on validation accuracy.

\subsection{The intervention}

$\lam \in \{0,\,0.25,\,0.5,\,1,\,2,\,4,\,8\}$; five seeds at
$\lam \in \{0, 2, 8\}$ and three at the interior points, for 27 runs.
Everything else is held fixed, so $\lam$ is the only manipulated variable.

\subsection{Measurements}

\paragraph{Domain leakage.} Logistic probe on frozen features predicting the
source dataset, reported as \textbf{balanced accuracy, whose chance floor is
$0.5$ for this two-domain problem}. Earlier work on this dataset pair reports
plain accuracy against a majority floor of $0.688$; the two scales must not be
compared to each other. Both are stated where relevant.

\paragraph{OOD detection.} Maximum softmax probability \cite{hendrycks2017},
free energy \cite{liu2020}, maximum cosine to a class centroid,
class-conditional Mahalanobis with shared covariance \cite{lee2018}, and $k$-NN
distance \cite{sun2022} ($k \in \{10,50,200\}$). All statistics are fit on the ISIC training
split alone. \textbf{These five rules are not independent evidence}: the
classifier is a linear map from $z_{\ell}^{\mathrm{norm}}$, so all five are
functions of the same 16-dimensional vector. Independent evidence comes from
repeating the analysis on the pre-projection backbone representation
(Section~\ref{sec:nonmediation}).

\paragraph{Calibration.} Expected calibration error, 15 bins, computed
separately on ISIC test and on \texttt{pad\_heldout} restricted to the six
shared classes. \textbf{Note that out-of-domain ECE here is arithmetically
close to mean confidence minus accuracy}, so the confidence, accuracy and ECE
results in Section~\ref{sec:confidently} are three views of one phenomenon,
not three independent findings.

\paragraph{Uncertainty.} Intervals on AUROC come from a stratified bootstrap with
2{,}000 resamples, percentile method, fixed in a pre-registration before the values
were computed. Each draw resamples \textbf{patients} from \texttt{pad\_heldout} and,
independently, images from the ISIC test split, because AUROC depends on both score
distributions and resampling only one side understates the interval. Within a draw the
same resampled sets are used for every seed. We report both the interval on the
five-seed mean and the number of seeds whose individual interval clears $0.5$.
Fitzpatrick17k carries no patient identifiers, so its bootstrap is over images and its
interval is likely anti-conservative.

\paragraph{Cross-domain diagnosis.} Zero-shot evaluation on
\texttt{pad\_heldout} over the six shared classes. PAD labels were never used
for $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cls}}$.

\subsection{Invariance is achieved, and in-distribution metrics do not warn}

Domain leakage falls from $0.915$ to $0.553$ between $\lam = 0$ and $\lam = 0.25$,
against a chance floor of $0.5$. By the smallest non-zero weight tested, the lesion
representation is very nearly uninformative about the acquisition source: the intended
effect of the method, obtained essentially in full.

It is obtained without an in-distribution cost. Balanced accuracy on the ISIC test
split moves from $0.692$ to $0.707$ over the useful range --- we claim no improvement,
the difference being small relative to seed variance --- and in-distribution expected
calibration error is flat at $\approx 0.10$ at every $\lam$. A practitioner tracking
accuracy, in-distribution calibration and the leakage probe would conclude that the
method had worked exactly as advertised.

\subsection{Out-of-distribution detection collapses, and inverts}

Over the same interval, Mahalanobis AUROC against the held-out PAD partition falls from
$0.843$ to $0.513$, and reaches $0.427$ at $\lam = 2$.

Two features of this collapse matter more than its size.

\paragraph{The fall to chance is a cliff; the inversion develops after it.} Almost the
whole descent happens in one step: $0.843$ to $0.509$ between $\lam = 0$ and
$\lam = 0.25$. The sub-chance region is reached only at $\lam \geq 0.5$, after which the
value moves between $0.427$ and $0.494$ with no ordering that would let a practitioner
tune for a safe compromise. We therefore distinguish the two: detection collapses to
chance at the smallest non-zero weight tested, and inverts under further pressure. There is no operating point on this curve at which
invariance has been obtained and detection survives.

\paragraph{It goes below chance.} At $\lam = 2$ the detector ranks images from a clinic
the model has never seen as \emph{more} in-distribution than images from its own test
split. A patient-clustered bootstrap puts the $95\%$ interval at $[0.414, 0.439]$, and
all five seeds have individual intervals entirely below $0.5$. The same holds for
$k$-NN ($0.440$, $[0.429, 0.452]$, $5/5$ seeds) and, with one seed short of unanimity,
for cosine ($0.453$, $[0.441, 0.464]$, $4/5$). This is not a tail artefact. Removing the pooled $90$th, $95$th or $99$th
percentile of scores leaves AUROC at $0.412$, $0.416$ and $0.424$ respectively against
$0.427$ for the full set, and the medians themselves invert (ISIC $13.8$ versus
\texttt{pad\_heldout} $10.0$). The shift is distributional throughout.

All five scoring rules move together --- MSP $0.970 \to 0.437$, energy
$0.983 \to 0.441$, cosine $0.901 \to 0.435$, $k$-NN $0.941 \to 0.424$ (stable across
$k \in \{10, 50, 200\}$). As noted in the methods these are five views of one
$16$-dimensional representation and are not independent evidence; the independent
replication is at the backbone (Section~\ref{sec:nonmediation}) and in the
single-encoder model (Section~\ref{sec:arch}).

The collapse is not a numerical artefact of the covariance estimate. Ledoit--Wolf
shrinkage leaves the $\lam = 2$ value unchanged on the held-out partition ($0.427$ under
both). Across float32 and float64 covariance estimation, with and without shrinkage, and
at one, four and eight BLAS threads, the largest deviation in AUROC is
$9.4 \times 10^{-4}$. Effective
dimensionality barely moves across the sweep (participation ratio $4.879 \to 4.500$
while leakage falls $0.914 \to 0.553$).

It is not an artefact of an unusually aggressive adversary. The adversary runs at
$30\times$ the base learning rate, and retraining at $10\times$ and $100\times$ leaves
the inversion in place: $0.418 \pm 0.011$ at $10\times$ with all three seeds below
$0.5$, against $0.432 \pm 0.024$ at $30\times$ and $0.482 \pm 0.020$ at $100\times$
(one seed at $0.503$). Weakening the adversary tenfold deepens the inversion rather
than removing it.

It is not confined to the domain the adversary was trained against. On Fitzpatrick17k,
absent from every training branch, AUROC falls $0.764 \to 0.391$ across the same sweep
($95\%$ CI $[0.386, 0.396]$, five of five seeds below $0.5$; the bootstrap here is over
images, since Fitzpatrick17k carries no patient identifiers, so the interval is likely
narrower than the true sampling uncertainty).
For context, a ResNet-50 baseline reaches $0.994$ on these images and the
EfficientNet-B3 single-encoder control $0.649$; the controlled comparison is the
$\lam = 0$ row, since it differs from $\lam = 2$ only in the adversarial weight.

\subsection{The model becomes confidently wrong on unseen domains}
\label{sec:confidently}

At $\lam = 0$ the model is appropriately hesitant on unfamiliar data: mean
maximum-softmax confidence is $0.915$ on the ISIC test split and $0.489$ on
\texttt{pad\_heldout}. By $\lam = 0.25$ that gap has closed ($0.913$ versus $0.898$),
and by $\lam = 2$ it has inverted --- the model is \emph{more} confident on images from
a clinic it has never seen ($0.930$) than on its own held-out test set ($0.910$).

Confidence rises while accuracy falls. Cross-domain diagnostic accuracy does not
improve at any $\lam$: balanced accuracy spans $0.249$--$0.291$ against a six-class
floor of $0.167$, no setting exceeds the $0.291$ obtained at $\lam = 0$, and every
setting is below the $0.298$ of the non-adversarial EfficientNet-B3 control. Plain
accuracy falls $0.245 \to 0.184$, beneath the $0.385$ majority-class rate throughout.

Macro AUC is roughly preserved ($0.605 \to 0.581$), so discrimination survives while
the decision rule does not. Predictions collapse toward the benign majority class:
nevus predictions rise from $113$ to $374$ of $716$ images, and \textbf{actinic
keratosis recall falls from $0.244$ to $0.016$} ($49/\nAK \to 3/\nAK$ images, mean over
five seeds). Squamous cell carcinoma moves the same way ($0.088 \to 0.021$,
$6/\nSCC \to 1/\nSCC$) but the count is too small to carry a claim on its own, and
melanoma ($n = \nMEL$) supports none.

The resulting out-of-domain calibration error triples, from $0.247$ to $0.746$, while
in-distribution ECE stays flat at $\approx 0.10$. That adapted models are miscalibrated
on the target domain is known \cite{wang2020}; what the sweep adds is that the
degradation is dose-dependent on the adversarial weight and leaves in-distribution
calibration untouched, so no in-distribution measurement reveals it. We stress that confidence, accuracy
and calibration here are three views of a single phenomenon rather than three
independent results: in this setting out-of-domain ECE is arithmetically close to mean
confidence minus accuracy.

\subsection{Leakage does not mediate the collapse}
\label{sec:nonmediation}

The natural hypothesis is that removing domain information is what breaks detection.
Three results rule it out.

First, leakage \textbf{saturates at its chance floor by $\lam = 0.25$} ($0.553$ against
a floor of $0.5$) while AUROC continues to fall from $0.513$ to $0.427$ as $\lam$ rises
further. The damage continues after invariance is complete.

Second, at the pre-projection backbone, leakage is essentially flat across the whole
sweep ($0.982 \to 0.941$) while backbone Mahalanobis AUROC falls $0.749 \to 0.475$.
Domain information remains linearly decodable there, and detection collapses anyway.
Because the backbone is a different representation from the $16$-dimensional latent,
this is the independent replication that the five-detector agreement does not provide.

Third, and most directly, two training objectives traverse the same leakage range with
opposite consequences (Section~\ref{sec:adversarial}). Leakage alone therefore does not
determine the outcome.

We accordingly report leakage as a measured quantity and never as the explanatory
variable, and plot all results against $\lam$ rather than against leakage.

Only MMD moves leakage appreciably, and it does so at a price gradient reversal never

reaches the extreme of the same pattern: at weight $100$ its balanced accuracy collapses
to $0.167$ while leakage remains at $0.950$. CORAL does not induce invariance at all;
at weight $100$ it makes the representation \emph{more} domain-separable
(leakage $0.998$). GroupDRO leaves leakage unmoved at every weight tested.

The contrast is cleanest when task health is held constant rather than leakage, since
MMD only reaches comparable leakage by destroying the classifier:

\begin{quote}
Gradient reversal reduces leakage $0.970 \to 0.763$ \textbf{at no cost in accuracy}
($0.845 \to 0.845$) and collapses all five detectors to chance. MMD reduces leakage
$0.967 \to 0.900$ at a similarly negligible cost ($0.779 \to 0.730$) and collapses
nothing (Mahalanobis $0.813 \to 0.716$). To go further, MMD must destroy the task.
\end{quote}

The calibration signatures differ in the same direction. As MMD degrades, its
out-of-domain ECE \emph{falls} ($0.344 \to 0.142$): the model becomes less confident as
it becomes less accurate, which is the appropriate response. Under gradient reversal
out-of-domain ECE triples while accuracy falls. The two objectives move leakage the same
way and fail in opposite manners --- one degrades honestly, the other is confidently
wrong.

We do not claim MMD would be safe at the invariance level gradient reversal reaches; it
never got there. What the data establish is that at the leakage levels MMD does reach,
it shows none of the failure, while gradient reversal at the same levels has already
collapsed.

the single-encoder must destroy it. The architecture that achieves deeper invariance
also suffers the larger collapse ($0.427$ versus $0.509$ at $\lam = 2$), which is the
dose--response relationship seen from a second direction.

\subsection{The collapse generalises to a same-modality site shift; the inversion does not}
\label{sec:site}

Every result above contrasts dermoscopy with clinical photography, so the construct we
call domain is confounded with imaging modality. ISIC~2019 contains a contrast that is
not: its \texttt{lesion\_id} field records the contributing site, with 12{,}413 images
from Hospital Cl\'inic Barcelona (\texttt{BCN}) and 10{,}015 from the HAM10000
collection (\texttt{HAM}). Two hospitals, two dermatoscopes, two patient populations ---
one modality.

\paragraph{The sites are separable, and not for trivial reasons.}
A logistic probe on frozen ERM features distinguishes them at balanced accuracy
$0.962 \pm 0.007$ against a floor of $0.5$, comparable to the $0.970$ that
ISIC$\leftrightarrow$PAD reaches at $\lam = 0$. Masking the dermatoscope vignette
reduces this only to $0.943 \pm 0.005$, so the border is not the signal. Hand-crafted
colour and intensity statistics reach $0.901$ at best --- close to the $0.876$ ceiling
the same features reach on ISIC$\leftrightarrow$PAD, so this pair is no more
low-level than the original contrast --- while sharpness statistics sit at chance
($0.532$). A probe given only the ground-truth class label reaches $0.668$, reflecting
the two sites' different class distributions; the feature probe is far above that
reference.

\paragraph{The collapse reproduces.}
Training single-encoder DANN with \texttt{BCN} as the in-distribution set and a
lesion-disjoint $30\%$ \texttt{HAM} partition held out, leakage falls from
$0.963 \pm 0.006$ to $0.821 \pm 0.030$ at $\lam = 0.25$, and the detectors that have
headroom fall through chance: $k$-NN $0.658 \pm 0.041 \to 0.486 \pm 0.009$ and cosine
$0.576 \pm 0.040 \to 0.477 \pm 0.006$ (3 seeds). The leakage reduction matches the
single-encoder dermatology sweep almost exactly ($0.970 \to 0.763$), and unlike the
two multi-domain settings in the limitations, the manipulation took.

Mahalanobis is uninformative in this direction: it reads $0.379$ at $\lam = 0$, already
below chance before any adversarial training. Reweighting the in-distribution set to the
out-of-domain class mix raises it to $0.487$, so most of the baseline inversion is class
composition --- HAM is two-thirds nevus and therefore concentrated near a single
well-populated centroid --- with a residual we attribute to HAM10000's more
standardised acquisition and correspondingly lower feature variance.

\paragraph{With the roles reversed, the inversion does not appear.}
Taking \texttt{HAM} as the in-distribution set restores the headroom: Mahalanobis reads
$0.846 \pm 0.003$ at $\lam = 0$, essentially identical to the $0.843$ of the
dermatology sweep. Adversarial training then reduces leakage from $0.95$ to $0.77$ and
drives Mahalanobis to $0.719 \pm 0.019$ at $\lam = 0.25$ and $0.683 \pm 0.015$ at
$\lam = 1$. The class-reweighted variant falls from $0.74$ to $0.54$. Detection degrades
substantially --- and stops above chance: at $\lam = 1$ the patient-clustered bootstrap
gives a $95\%$ interval of $[0.666,\, 0.699]$, and all three seeds have individual
intervals lying entirely above $0.5$.

\paragraph{Reading.}
The comparison is tighter than it first appears. Baseline detectability is the same
($0.846$ versus $0.843$) and the achieved invariance is the same as the single-encoder
dermatology run ($0.77$ versus $0.763$), yet the cross-modality contrast reaches $0.427$
while the same-modality contrast stops at $0.683$. Neither a shortage of headroom nor a
shortfall in achieved invariance accounts for the difference.

We therefore separate two claims that the earlier sections report together. The
\emph{collapse} --- detection falling to chance as invariance is obtained, invisibly to
in-distribution metrics --- reproduces under a same-modality shift between two
hospitals. The \emph{inversion} does not: in every setting where we observe it, the two
domains differ in imaging modality. Whether that reflects a threshold in the size of the
shift, something specific to modality gaps, or a property of these particular datasets,
we cannot say.

\subsection{A domain monitor is cheap, and semantic novelty is unaffected}

A linear domain head on a frozen ImageNet ResNet-50 that has never seen this data
reaches AUROC $0.998$; on the trained baseline, $0.997$; on the trained EfficientNet-B3
control, $0.991$. Detecting the acquisition domain is easy from almost any
representation, so retaining a monitor is not in itself difficult. What distinguishes
the dual-encoder's context branch is \emph{concentration}: a single principal component
carries $84.5\%$ of its variance and already separates the domains
(AUROC $>0.9999$, two discordant pairs in $11.6$M), against $0.55$--$0.70$ at $k = 1$
for every other representation tested.

The damage is specific to covariate shift. With the domain held constant and diagnostic
classes withheld from training instead, both branches land between $0.6$ and $0.8$ and
the ordering depends on which class is withheld (DF and VASC: lesion $0.648$, context
$0.641$, baseline $0.687$; SCC: lesion $0.806$, context $0.607$, baseline $0.684$). No
near/far structure organises the difference (Pearson $-0.12$ against a
centroid-similarity index). We report this as a null result.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{An inversion, not a degradation}

The result we consider central is not that out-of-distribution detection weakens
under adversarial invariance training. It is that the ordering reverses. At
$\lam = 2$, a model asked which images are unlike its training data answers with
its own test split, and ranks images from a clinic it has never seen as the more
routine of the two. Every scoring rule we tried agrees, the effect holds on a
third dataset the adversary never saw, and it survives shrinkage estimation and
truncation of the score distribution's tails.

Degradation would be unsurprising: a representation stripped of domain
information should be less able to recognise a domain. Chance performance is
what the obvious argument predicts, and chance is what a single-encoder model
reaches at $\lam = 0.25$ ($0.525$). Going \emph{past} chance is not predicted by
that argument, and we cannot currently explain it.

\subsection{The deployment pathway}

The results compose into a concrete failure sequence. A model is trained with
invariance to acquisition domain, as recommended, and deployed at a new site. On
images from that site the model increasingly predicts the benign majority class,
with actinic keratosis recall falling from $0.244$ to $0.016$ ($49$ of $\nAK$
images correctly recalled, then $3$). It does so with higher confidence than on
its own test data. The monitor that should flag those images for human review
scores them as more routine than familiar ones --- not merely failing to flag
them, but actively ranking them as safer. And the developer's evaluation shows
in-distribution accuracy holding, in-distribution calibration unchanged, and the
leakage metric improved exactly as intended.

\subsection{The failure belongs to gradient reversal, not to invariance}

Reducing domain leakage is not sufficient to produce the failure. MMD reduces
leakage along the same axis and collapses nothing: at the leakage levels it
reaches with the classifier intact, every detector remains well above chance.
Gradient reversal at comparable leakage has already inverted.

The two objectives also fail in opposite manners. As MMD is pushed harder it
becomes less accurate \emph{and less confident}, and its out-of-domain
calibration error falls. Under gradient reversal accuracy falls while confidence
rises, and calibration error triples. One degrades honestly; the other is
confidently wrong. Whatever drives the inversion is a property of how invariance is obtained, not of
invariance itself. We tested one adversarial formulation. Whether other adversarial
objectives --- CDAN, adversarial discriminative adaptation --- behave like gradient
reversal or like the moment-matching objectives is not established here, so the claim
is stated for gradient reversal rather than for adversarial training in general.

We cannot rule out that MMD would behave the same way at the invariance level
gradient reversal reaches, because it never got there without destroying the
task. The claim is bounded accordingly.

\subsection{The danger zone is where the method succeeds}

Risk scales with \emph{achieved} invariance, not with the nominal weight or with
the presence of an adversarial term in the loss. In a separate setting we ran
the same objective with an adversary that never learned; leakage stayed at
$0.94$ across four orders of magnitude of $\lam$, and the safety failure did not
appear either. A weak adversary produces neither debiasing nor harm. Many
deployed configurations may be too weak to induce invariance, and are
correspondingly harmless --- which also means that a configuration's safety
cannot be read off its hyperparameters.

\subsection{Tolerance without generalisation}

Full-spectrum OOD detection \cite{yang2023} prescribes that covariate-shifted
in-distribution inputs be accepted rather than flagged. Our models achieve that
prescription and are unsafe because of it. By $\lam = 2$ they accept
out-of-domain images more readily than their own test data, while classifying
them at $0.249$ balanced accuracy against a floor of $0.167$ and reporting
higher confidence than in distribution.

Acceptance and competence are separate properties, and current protocols
evaluate them separately or not at all. We suggest that any claim of tolerance
to covariate shift be accompanied by cross-domain accuracy on the same inputs,
since tolerating inputs a model cannot classify is a failure mode rather than a
success.

\subsection{Two routes to invariance are not interchangeable}

Wald et al.~\cite{wald2021} showed that enforcing multi-domain calibration
produces invariance. We find the converse fails: obtaining invariance
adversarially destroys out-of-domain calibration, driving ECE from $0.247$ to
$0.746$. The property one route uses as its foundation is the property the other
route removes.

This suggests a design preference. Where invariance is the goal,
calibration-based or moment-matching objectives may be preferable to adversarial
ones, because they reach invariance through constraints that leave the
out-of-domain uncertainty signal intact. Our MMD results are consistent with
this, but we have not tested it directly and offer it as a hypothesis the
present results motivate.

\subsection{Recommendation}

Report OOD detection and out-of-domain calibration \textbf{as a function of
achieved invariance}, not at a single operating point; report cross-domain
accuracy alongside any claim of tolerance to covariate shift; and state, when a
separate dataset serves as the OOD set, that this measures covariate shift
rather than semantic novelty. Practitioners tuning $\lam$ already train at
multiple values, so this is a change in what is reported rather than additional
work. It converts an invisible failure into a visible number.

\subsection{An open mechanism}

Six accounts were tested and refuted and a pre-registered synthetic reproduction
failed. We do not know why the inversion occurs. We know that it is a bulk
location shift rather than a tail effect, that it survives shrinkage estimation,
that it is not mediated by linear domain-decodability, that it appears on
domains the adversary never saw, that it reproduces without the factorized
architecture, and that moment-matching objectives do not produce it. We report
the eliminations so that the search space is smaller for whoever resolves it.

\section{Limitations}

\textbf{Absolute cross-domain performance is poor for every model here.} Plain
accuracy on \texttt{pad\_heldout} is $0.245$ for the factorized model and
$0.391$ for the baseline, against a $0.385$ majority rate. No model in this
study is deployable. Our claim concerns the \emph{effect of $\lam$} within a
controlled intervention, not the deployability of any particular model, and the
deployment pathway above should be read as a mechanism of harm rather than as a
description of a fielded system.

\textbf{The inversion is observed only across imaging modality.} ISIC is dermoscopy;
PAD-UFES and Fitzpatrick17k are clinical photography, so the main contrast confounds
site with modality, and Fitzpatrick17k does not resolve this because it is also
clinical photography. The same-modality contrast within ISIC
(Section~\ref{sec:site}) separates the two claims: the collapse reproduces between two
hospitals, the inversion does not. Every setting in which we observe the inversion is a
cross-modality one, and we cannot say whether that reflects the size of the shift, a
property of modality gaps specifically, or these particular datasets.

Two attempts to replicate outside dermatology failed for different reasons, and
neither settles the question. On Camelyon17 the adversary head never trained:
cross-entropy sat at $\ln 3$ from the first epoch at every $\lam$ while a
post-hoc probe on the same features read $0.94$, despite a gradient graph
verified correct (head cosine $+1.000$, encoder $-1.000$). On iWildCam the
adversary did train --- best-epoch cross-entropy $3.13$ against $\ln 243 =
5.49$ --- but the encoder never became invariant: the binary train-versus-OOD
site probe moved only from $0.911$ to $0.891$ across $\lam \in [0, 10]$, against
a floor of $0.5$, and no detector collapsed. \textbf{We therefore report no
result from either dataset, and make no claim that either resists invariance.}
In both cases the manipulation did not take, so there was nothing to measure.

One observation survives these failures and may be useful to others. Invariance
was obtained easily in the two-domain dermatology setting --- leakage fell from
$0.970$ to $0.763$ at $\lam = 0.25$ --- and was not obtained at any weight in
either multi-domain setting. Adversarial invariance may be substantially harder
to achieve as the number of training domains grows, which would bound the
settings in which the failure reported here can be observed at all. We offer
this as an observation rather than a claim; it rests on two negative results.

Generality beyond dermatology remains untested.

\textbf{Backbone coverage.} Comparable invariance was not reached with a ResNet-50
backbone, so whether the inversion extends beyond EfficientNet-B3 is untested. Three of
the nine runs failed their adversary gate outright and are reported as null experiments
rather than negative results.

\textbf{The objective comparison is bounded.} MMD never reached the invariance
level gradient reversal attains; it could only approach it by destroying the
classifier. We therefore cannot say what moment-matching would do at matched
invariance, only that at the leakage levels it reaches, gradient reversal has
already inverted and MMD has not.

\textbf{Detector non-independence.} The five scoring rules are functions of the
same 16-dimensional latent. The backbone analysis and the single-encoder
replication provide the independent evidence.

\textbf{Calibration arithmetic.} Out-of-domain ECE is close to mean confidence
minus accuracy in this setting; the confidence, accuracy and ECE results are one
phenomenon viewed three ways, not three independent findings.

\textbf{Seeds.} Five seeds at the endpoints, three at interior points. Effect
sizes are large relative to seed variance, but formal testing across seven
points at $n = 3$ is not claimed.

\textbf{Splits.} ISIC splits are image-level, not patient-level; some patients
may contribute images to both partitions. Because in-distribution accuracy is
compared \emph{across $\lam$ under an identical split}, any inflation applies
equally to every row and does not threaten the reported pattern, though absolute
values may be optimistic. PAD splits are patient-level, disjoint by patient and
by lesion.

\textbf{Third-domain coverage.} 3{,}887 of 16{,}577 Fitzpatrick17k images were
obtained; the set is web-scraped under mixed copyright, used for evaluation only,
and not redistributed.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Conclusion}

Under domain-adversarial training, out-of-distribution detection does not merely
weaken. It reverses: a model asked which inputs are unfamiliar answers with its
own test data and ranks a new clinic's images as the more routine of the two.
The reversal appears at the smallest adversarial weight that achieves
invariance, holds on a dataset the adversary never saw, reproduces without the
factorized architecture, and does not appear under moment-matching objectives
that reduce leakage along the same axis.

It is invisible to everything a developer monitors. In-distribution accuracy
holds, in-distribution calibration is unchanged, and the domain leakage probe
improves exactly as intended, while confidence on unseen domains overtakes
confidence on familiar data and recall for a premalignant class falls to
$0.016$.

We could not explain the inversion after refuting six candidate accounts and a
pre-registered synthetic reproduction. Explaining it is not required to act on
it: reporting out-of-distribution detection, out-of-domain calibration and
cross-domain accuracy as functions of achieved invariance costs almost nothing
and makes the failure visible.
